1. Why This Report Now

Telecommunications is not just another commercial sector. It is foundational critical national infrastructure: emergency services, government operations, financial systems, healthcare delivery, energy control, transportation, and public-safety communications all depend on it. When telecommunications degrades or fails, everything downstream degrades with it. The cybersecurity posture of the US telecommunications sector is, therefore, a public-interest condition before it is a commercial one.

Conventional cybersecurity reporting in the sector tends to ask internal questions: are controls deployed, are systems patched, were audits passed. Those questions matter at the operator level. But they do not, in aggregate, describe what publicly available information shows about the state of the sector as a whole — which is, increasingly, the lens through which regulators, boards and the public form a view of telecommunications cybersecurity. This report addresses the latter question.

The US telecommunications sector is unusually fragmented at the operator level. Approximately 90% of providers in the FCC registry are privately held; a clear majority generate annual revenue below ten million dollars; the typical provider employs fewer than fifty people. The combination of a long tail of small operators, rising threat activity and increasing regulatory expectation produces a sector-level risk picture materially different from the picture in better-known critical-infrastructure sectors such as finance or energy.

The reasons to bring sector-level visibility to this picture have intensified materially in the past 18 months. In late 2024, US federal authorities publicly disclosed that a sustained, state-aligned cyber-espionage campaign — referred to in public reporting and in subsequent CISA, FBI and NSA advisories under the designation "Salt Typhoon" — had compromised the networks of major US telecommunications providers and had achieved persistent access for the purposes of intelligence collection. A joint advisory issued by CISA, the FBI, the NSA and partner-nation agencies on 27 August 2025 (AA25-239A) characterized the activity as targeting telecommunications, government, transportation, lodging and military networks, with edge devices such as routers, firewalls and VPN appliances as primary points of initial access, frequently through known, unpatched vulnerabilities.

The volume picture is consistent. The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center reported, in its 2024 Annual Report, total cybercrime losses of approximately $16.6 billion across more than 859,000 complaints — a 33% year-over-year increase and a record high — and described ransomware as the most pervasive threat to critical infrastructure. Independent industry research from Verizon (DBIR), IBM (Cost of a Data Breach), Mandiant (M-Trends) and Sophos (State of Ransomware) points in the same direction.

The findings in this report are designed to be useful at three distinct levels. At the industry level, they make it possible to distinguish risk patterns that are isolated to particular operators from risk patterns that are endemic to the sector — the latter typically requiring policy, procurement or standards-level action rather than only individual remediation. At the state and regional level, the same findings can be correlated with public-safety dependencies — 911 and Next-Gen 911 routing, emergency-alert systems, rural infrastructure dependency — turning a cybersecurity picture into a public-safety one. At the operator level, the indicator categories described in this report align with categories already monitored in well-developed cybersecurity programs, and provide a structured reference point for those still being built out.

This report reflects a single point in time, and the picture it presents will change. The intent over time is to convert this snapshot into a longitudinal record — one in which boards, regulators and operators can read not only the current condition of the sector but the rate and direction in which it is changing. The signal that matters most to the long-term integrity of US telecommunications is not "controls were implemented" but "measurable risk has fallen, year over year."

It is against that backdrop that this report is published. Its purpose is to support sector-level awareness, board-level discussion, regulatory engagement and policy formulation — at a sufficient level of detail to inform those discussions, while remaining strictly at the industry level rather than at the individual-operator level.

2. Approach and Sources

This report draws on publicly available information about the US telecommunications industry. The principal reference set is the FCC's public telecommunications provider registry, supplemented by openly published business intelligence on the providers identified in that registry and contextualised against established third-party industry research. The objective is to characterise systemic cyber risk signals affecting the sector at the industry level, with emphasis on structural, economic and disclosure-driven dynamics relevant to policy and program oversight. No organisation-level attribution is offered.

Data sources. Only publicly accessible sources are used — no non-public, investigative or privileged data. Sources include:

The FCC public telecommunications provider registry

Government-maintained vulnerability repositories and advisories

Security advisories issued by technology and equipment vendors

Public vulnerability disclosures aligned to common enumeration schemes (e.g. CVE)

Publicly reported incidents, enforcement summaries and regulatory filings

Openly published industry research, including the Verizon DBIR series and CISA advisories

Analytical universe. The base comprises 4,975 active US telecommunications providers, of which 3,106 were taken forward into the detailed industry analysis, and of which 2,132 carry a fully enriched profile combining business and risk-indicator information. Throughout this report, percentages and counts are reported in relation to the relevant denominator within that universe — typically the 3,106 telcos that form the analytical base, or the 2,132 telcos for which enriched segmentation cuts are possible.

Indicators. Risk indicators referenced in this report are widely-used industry concepts — for example, presence of CVEs catalogued by CISA as actively exploited, posture of email authentication records (DMARC and SPF) as published in DNS, and the relative scale of an operator's public web presence. These indicators are common reference points across multiple cybersecurity research publications.

Processing and aggregation. Collected disclosures are normalised to address duplication across sources, categorised by technology class or functional dependency, and evaluated over time to identify recurring patterns. Outputs are presented using aggregated statistics, ranges and trend indicators; geographic reporting is generalised where necessary; organisation-specific details are excluded from all public outputs. The methodology does not independently verify third-party disclosures or assess remediation status.

Known limitations. Findings should be interpreted as directional indicators of systemic risk, not as deterministic measures of any provider's security posture. Three factors constrain the analysis:

Variability in disclosure practices across vendors and operators

Time lags between discovery, disclosure and public reporting

Absence of visibility into undisclosed vulnerabilities or mitigations

Regulatory context. This analysis complements — not replaces — existing regulatory, grant-oversight and program frameworks. It highlights cross-cutting vulnerability conditions, informs guidance and technical-assistance strategies, and supports resilience considerations in telecommunications policy. It is not intended to establish new regulatory expectations or interpret existing ones.

Findings should be read as industry-level observations rather than as assertions about any individual provider. Aggregate counts and percentages are intended to illuminate sector-wide patterns; they are not intended to enable, and should not be used to attempt, the identification of any specific operator. Further notes on the legal and methodological framing of this report appear in Appendix A.

3. The Structure of the US Telecommunications Industry

Before turning to risk, the report establishes the structural shape of the industry, because that shape determines the operating context in which risk needs to be understood.

3.1 An Overwhelmingly Small Private Industry Operations Environment

Across the 4,975 telcos in the analytical universe, approximately 90% are privately held. Public companies — primarily the large incumbent groups and a handful of cable conglomerates — represent around 8% of the population. The remainder are cooperatives, municipal utilities and a small group of public-benefit corporations operating broadband as a civic service. The industry is, by entity count, a long tail of independent operators.

Revenue distribution reinforces this. The single largest revenue band among the telcos in the analysis is $1M–$10M, capturing 35.7% of providers. Adding the under-$1M band, more than half the industry — 51.7% — operates below the $10M annual revenue line. Only 5.9% of providers report annual revenue above one billion dollars, and these account for the household incumbent names that dominate national consumer awareness but are a numerical minority within the FCC registry.

Headcount tells the same story. Approximately 69% of telcos employ fewer than fifty people. Roughly a third operate with ten or fewer staff. These are organizations in which cybersecurity, networking, customer support and billing are typically managed by the same one or two technical staff.

3.2 Wireless Internet Providers Outnumber Cable Operators

Among providers with a clear classification, Wireless Internet Service Providers (WISPs) total 1,988 — outnumbering cable operators (756) by a factor of approximately 2.6 to 1. WISPs are the dominant rural-broadband delivery model in the US: small, fixed-wireless operators serving sparse populations underserved by fibre or cable. Texas alone hosts 207 WISPs; California, 176; Florida, 148. Iowa, with 65 WISPs and 67 cable operators among 205 total telcos, is unusually dense relative to population — a pattern consistent with decades of rural cooperative investment in independent connectivity.

3.3 Voice over IP Is Now the Largest Service Class

Across the FCC's eighteen principal communications-type categories, Interconnected VoIP leads with 2,109 providers (42.4%), followed by Incumbent Local Exchange Carriers at 974 (19.6%) and competitive local exchange carriers at 576 (11.6%). The shift away from traditional Public Switched Telephone Network operations is now visible at the registry level: a clear plurality of US telcos in 2026 derive their primary FCC classification from internet-based voice rather than legacy wireline.

MARKET STRUCTURE TAKEAWAY

The US telecommunications industry, by entity count, is not a small group of giants. It is a large, fragmented community of small private operators — WISPs, ILECs, VoIP providers and cooperatives — most below ten million dollars in revenue and fewer than fifty staff. Any policy or risk framework that assumes telcos are well-resourced enterprises will fundamentally misread the median operator.

4.1 What Drives the Elevated Risk Band

Across the 1,754 telcos in the Critical band, the most prevalent contributing factors are widely-recognized industry indicators:

Presence of CVEs that appear on CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue — 1,195 telcos (38.5% of the analyzed base).

Presence of CVEs with Exploit Prediction Scoring System (EPSS) values that indicate elevated likelihood of exploitation — 968 telcos (31.2%).

Subdomain takeover risk associated with dangling DNS configuration — 1,266 telcos (40.8%); the most widespread single indicator by telco count.

Indicators consistent with public-facing remote-access service configuration — present at meaningful rates across the analyzed base.

Indicators consistent with public-facing database service configuration — present at lower but still material rates.

Web frontend vulnerability indicators consistent with outdated client-side libraries — present across a third of the analyzed base.

KEY TAKEAWAY

The elevated risk band is not driven by exotic or sophisticated vulnerabilities. It is driven by ordinary, well-documented exposures — KEV-listed CVEs that have been public for years, configuration weaknesses in DNS and email authentication, and outdated client-side software. Every category listed is addressable through configuration discipline rather than capital investment.

5.2 Email Authentication Posture

Email authentication configuration is among the most measurable and most widely benchmarked dimensions of organizational cyber posture. The DMARC and SPF standards have been industry-recommended for over a decade. Industry-level posture across the analytical base is as follows:

992 of the 3,106 telcos analyzed (31.9%) publish DMARC records configured weakly — typically with policy p=none, which provides telemetry to the operator but does not instruct receiving servers to reject anything. 1,293 telcos (41.6%) publish SPF records configured with the soft-fail qualifier, which receiving servers may interpret as advisory rather than enforcement. 496 telcos (16.0%) carry both weaknesses on the same domain — the configuration that makes domain spoofing operationally trivial.

The risk amplification for telecommunications providers specifically is significant. Internet service providers are high-trust senders to their customers — billing notices, service alerts and password reset emails from a customer's ISP are among the email types users are most likely to act on without scrutiny. The Verizon DBIR has, in successive editions, identified business email compromise and brand impersonation as among the fastest-growing categories of breach activity.

KEY TAKEAWAY

DMARC enforcement is a DNS configuration change, not a project. For nearly a thousand telcos in the analytical base, moving from p=none to p=reject is a single record edit. The persistence of weak DMARC at the industry level therefore appears more closely associated with awareness and ownership factors than with technical or budget constraints.

6. Infrastructure Posture Indicators

The industry analysis incorporated indicators related to the configuration of administrative, file-transfer, database and network management services associated with telco infrastructure. Aggregate observations are summarized below at the industry level; no specific operator, asset, or technical configuration is identified.

Remote-access service configuration is present at meaningful levels across the analytical base. Industry context: the Verizon DBIR has repeatedly identified compromised remote-access services as a leading initial-access vector in ransomware campaigns affecting small and mid-sized organizations.

Legacy file-transfer protocol configuration is present at higher rates than would be expected of a modernized industry — indicating retained legacy operational workflows in a meaningful share of telcos.

Database service configuration indicators are present at lower but still material rates. Public-facing database services without compensating controls represent a category of risk well documented in industry research, and one routinely cited in cybersecurity advisories as relevant to small and mid-sized operators.

Network management protocol configuration indicators are present at smaller but operationally significant rates. This category is referenced in industry research as a contributing factor in extended attacker dwell-time scenarios within affected networks.

6.1 DNS Configuration and Subdomain Takeover Risk

The single most widespread indicator across the analytical base — present in 1,266 of the 3,106 telcos analyzed (40.8%) — is dangling DNS configuration consistent with subdomain takeover risk. A dangling DNS record occurs when an organization publishes a DNS pointer to a third-party hosted resource (cloud storage, hosted application, content platform) and subsequently decommissions that resource without removing the DNS record.

An attacker who registers the abandoned third-party resource gains the ability to serve content from the operator's legitimate subdomain, often with a valid TLS certificate automatically issued by public certificate authorities. This category of risk is widely discussed in industry security publications and represents a particularly difficult risk for operators to detect internally, because it does not produce alerts in conventional vulnerability management or security information systems.

KEY TAKEAWAY

Dangling DNS configuration is the largest single risk indicator in the industry analysis by carrier count, and the one most invisible to operators. For a telecommunications provider, a successful subdomain takeover converts the operator's brand into a phishing platform without the operator's knowledge.

8. Why This Matters for the Industry

8.1 The Public-Safety Dimension

Telecommunications providers are not commercial entities like any other. For thousands of US telcos, their service is the substrate on which 911 calls travel, on which emergency alerts are delivered, and on which rural communities reach the rest of the country. A successful cybersecurity incident affecting a sole connectivity provider in a rural region can leave that region without internet access, without VoIP telephony, and — depending on the provider's role in the regional 911 architecture — without emergency dispatch capability. The 64.8% elevated-risk-band rate observed at the industry level is not solely a commercial risk number. It is also a public-resilience number.

The federal regulatory framework has begun to recognize this. FCC Customer Proprietary Network Information rules require breach notification within seven business days, with substantial per-violation penalties available. CISA has placed telecommunications in its critical-infrastructure sector list. State-level data-breach notification laws apply additionally in more than thirty US jurisdictions. But regulation alone cannot remediate weak DMARC records or unpatched SharePoint instances. The compliance overlay assumes that the provider has the operational capacity to fix the underlying issue — an assumption that is fragile for the approximately 69% of telcos that employ fewer than fifty people.

8.2 The Capital and Transactional Implication

For investors, lenders and acquirers active in the US telecommunications space, this report reframes the diligence question. A target's reported revenue, EBITDA and subscriber count tell one story; the underlying cyber-posture indicators on which this report is based tell another. With the elevated-risk-band rate sitting at 64.8% across the analyzed base, the cyber-exposure baseline on a randomly-selected target is approximately a coin flip toward the worst risk band. Industry research from sources including the IBM Cost of a Data Breach Report consistently indicates that small-operator cyber insurance is materially mispriced relative to realistic ransomware-recovery scenarios in the relevant revenue tier.

The same data also informs the consolidation thesis active in regional telecommunications markets. Roll-up plays in the small-operator end of the industry are operationally constrained not only by integration complexity but by the security-debt remediation required to bring acquired entities to a unified standard.

8.3 The Vendor Ecosystem Implication

The pattern of indicators surfaced by the industry analysis — recurring weakness in email authentication configuration, recurring presence of dangling DNS configuration, recurring presence of legacy operational protocols, recurring presence of long-standing CVE families — is consistent across operators in a way that suggests a small number of shared root causes rather than uncorrelated individual choices. Many small telcos use the same handful of billing platforms, OSS/BSS vendors and managed website providers. The analysis does not allow naming any specific vendor, but the clustering pattern is consistent with vendor-driven risk being a non-trivial share of the total. Industry associations and managed security service providers serving the small-telco market represent the most scalable lever for sector-wide risk reduction.

9. Recommendations and Industry Roadmap

9.1 Immediate Actions (0–30 Days)

Move every domain to DMARC p=reject. A single DNS record change addresses email authentication weakness for the meaningful share of operators currently publishing weak DMARC. No tooling, no budget, no project plan.

Restrict remote-access services to known address ranges only. Firewall configuration change. Removes the primary initial-access vector identified by Verizon DBIR and other industry-standard breach-research publications as most associated with ransomware activity.

Patch CISA Known Exploited Vulnerabilities. Prioritize the entries identified in Section 5, particularly the SharePoint, Exchange, BlueKeep and PHP-CGI families. CISA mandates this for federal agencies; industry best practice extends the same expectation to telecommunications operators.

Audit DNS configuration for dangling records. Highest-impact, lowest-cost action available for the largest single risk-indicator category in the analysis. A DNS audit can be run in hours; remediation is record-by-record.

9.2 Short-Term Actions (30–90 Days)

Move SPF records from soft-fail to hard-fail on all sending domains.

Disable cleartext mail and file-transfer protocols. Force encrypted equivalents.

Run a comprehensive web footprint inventory with explicit ownership assignment per asset.

Update widely-used client-side libraries (notably jQuery) to current supported versions across customer-facing properties.

Establish a DNS lifecycle policy that requires DNS cleanup as a sign-off step for any project decommissioning.

9.3 Strategic Actions (90+ Days)

Subscribe to or implement continuous attack-surface monitoring. Point-in-time analyses age out within weeks; ongoing visibility is now industry-standard practice.

Evaluate managed security service provider relationships for telcos in the sub-fifty-employee segment. Federal broadband programmes such as those administered by NTIA and USDA may co-fund security capability uplift for eligible rural providers.

Adopt vendor security service-level requirements. Where shared platforms (billing, OSS/BSS, managed websites) are used, require contractual breach notification timelines aligned to FCC CPNI rules.

Right-size cyber insurance coverage. For operators in the $1M–$10M revenue band, industry research consistently indicates that breach-recovery costs are typically underinsured by a factor of two-to-four times realistic ransomware-recovery scenarios.

1. 64.8% of the 3,106 US telcos in this analysis sit in an elevated cyber-risk band. This is the median condition of the industry, not the worst tail.

2. WISPs and cable operators are functionally indistinguishable on the headline cyber-risk indicators despite different business models — the conventional rural-versus-urban risk narrative is not supported by the data.

3. Web presence complexity is the dominant predictor of risk band. Operational complexity matters more than company size, age or revenue.

4. Dangling DNS configuration is the single largest risk indicator across the industry, present in 1,266 telcos (40.8%) — and the indicator most invisible to operators internally.

5. The lowest-effort, highest-yield improvements are configuration changes — DMARC enforcement, remote-access access control, DNS hygiene — not new tooling. The persistence of weak posture at the industry level therefore appears more closely associated with awareness and ownership factors than with budget constraints.
